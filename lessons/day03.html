Module 4: Phrasal Verbs

Stand out

Be noticeable or better than others.

Usage: Highlight a unique quality.

  • Interview: My attention to detail makes me stand out.
  • Office: His presentation stood out among the rest.
  • Tech: This framework stands out for its speed.

Step up

Take responsibility or act.

Usage: Showing initiative.

  • Interview: I stepped up when the manager was on leave.
  • Office: We need someone to step up and lead this.
  • Tech: I stepped up to fix the critical bug.

Come through

Succeed in doing what is expected.

Usage: Proving reliability.

  • Interview: I always come through on tight deadlines.
  • Office: The team came through with the report.
  • Tech: The hotfix came through just in time.

Rise to [a challenge]

Show ability to deal with a difficulty.

Usage: Demonstrating courage.

  • Interview: I rise to the challenge under pressure.
  • Office: She rose to the challenge of the new role.
  • Tech: We rose to the challenge of migrating servers.

Back up

Support with evidence or help.

Usage: Proving claims.

  • Interview: I can back up my claims with metrics.
  • Office: He backed up the team during crunch time.
  • Tech: Always back up your code before pushing.

Stand by

Support or remain loyal to a decision.

Usage: Showing conviction.

  • Interview: I stand by my design choices.
  • Office: The manager stood by her team.
  • Tech: We stand by our technical stack.

Build up

Develop or increase gradually.

Usage: Discussing skill acquisition.

  • Interview: I have built up my expertise over 5 years.
  • Office: We need to build up our team's morale.
  • Tech: I built up a library of reusable components.

Prove yourself

Show what you are capable of.

Usage: Describing ambition.

  • Interview: I am eager to prove myself in this role.
  • Office: She proved herself during the probation period.
  • Tech: The new algorithm proved itself in testing.

Hold your ground

Maintain your position/opinion.

Usage: Demonstrating assertiveness.

  • Interview: I can hold my ground in difficult negotiations.
  • Office: He held his ground during the budget meeting.
  • Tech: I held my ground on not releasing untested code.

Come across as

Give an impression.

Usage: Discussing communication style.

  • Interview: I aim to come across as confident and capable.
  • Office: She comes across as very approachable.
  • Tech: The error message comes across as confusing.

Module 6: Grammar - Modal Verbs of Ability

Modal verbs are auxiliary verbs that express necessity, possibility, ability, or permission.

Formula: Subject + Modal Verb + Base Form of Main Verb
ModalCore MeaningInterview Example
CanPresent abilityI can work effectively under pressure.
CouldPast ability / Polite capabilityI could implement this feature in React.
MayPossibility / PermissionI may require some initial domain training.
MightWeak possibility / TentativeI might suggest a microservices approach here.
ShouldRecommendation / ObligationYou should consider refactoring this module.
MustStrong obligation / DeductionWe must meet the deadline.
WillCertainty / WillingnessI will ensure the code is fully tested.
WouldConditional / Polite requestI would approach the problem systematically.
Ought toMoral obligationWe ought to document this properly.

Common Errors

Wrong: 'I am knowing how to code in Python.'
Correct: 'I know how to code in Python.'

Explanation: Modal verbs and stative verbs don't use -ing form.

Wrong: 'I can to solve this problem.'
Correct: 'I can solve this problem.'

Explanation: After modal verbs, always use base form WITHOUT 'to' (except ought to).

Wrong: 'I must to attend the meeting.'
Correct: 'I must attend the meeting.'

Explanation: 'Must' is followed directly by base verb form.

Exercises

1. I ___ (can/must) handle difficult clients effectively.

2. We ___ (should/would) optimize the database queries to improve speed.

3. I ___ (might/must) consider learning a new language this year.

Module 10: Reading Practice

The 5 Strengths Top Tech Companies Look For in Software Engineers

When interviewing for top tech companies, candidates often mistakenly believe that algorithms and coding speed are the only factors that matter. While technical proficiency is the baseline, hiring managers are actually looking for specific core strengths that indicate long-term success.

1. Systems Thinking. Great engineers don't just write functions; they understand how their code impacts the broader system. They can foresee edge cases and architectural bottlenecks.

2. Clear Communication. An engineer who can write brilliant code but cannot explain it to a product manager is a liability. The ability to articulate complex technical decisions clearly and concisely is paramount.

3. Intellectual Curiosity. Tech stacks evolve rapidly. Companies look for individuals who demonstrate a continuous desire to learn and adapt, diving deep into new frameworks without being asked.

4. Emotional Intelligence. Software development is a team sport. Being able to give and receive constructive feedback gracefully, navigate disagreements, and support peers is critical for maintaining team velocity.

5. Consistent Execution. Reliability beats sporadic brilliance. Managers highly value engineers who consistently deliver high-quality code on time, every time.

Questions:

  1. What is the 'baseline' requirement mentioned in the text?
  2. Why is clear communication described as paramount?
  3. How does the article define 'systems thinking'?
  4. Summarize the article in two sentences.

Throughout my five-year career in software engineering, my greatest strength has been my ability to combine deep technical expertise with strategic systems thinking. I specialize in designing scalable backend architectures using Node.js and AWS. A prime example of this was my recent project where I led the refactoring of our legacy monolithic application into microservices. By implementing a systematic and tenacious approach to debugging and optimization, I reduced server response times by 40% and infrastructure costs by 20%. Beyond my technical capabilities, I am a highly communicative and collaborative team member. I facilitate weekly knowledge-sharing sessions that bridge the gap between our engineering and product teams. I am confident that my unique blend of architectural foresight and clear communication would make me an exemplary addition to your engineering department.

Module 13: HR Interview Coaching

Question: 'What is your greatest strength?'

Bad Answer

"I am a hard worker and a perfectionist. I always do my best and I know Python really well."

(Generic, unquantified, no examples.)
Good Answer

"My greatest strength is my problem-solving ability. When our server crashed last month, I stayed late to figure out the bug and fix it. I am very dedicated to my work."

(One strength with one supporting example, but lacks depth.)
Excellent Answer

"My greatest strength is my ability to translate complex technical problems into clear, actionable strategies. For instance, in my last role, we faced a severe database bottleneck. Instead of applying a quick patch, I systematically analyzed the query logs, identified the root cause, and presented a comprehensive optimization plan to the non-technical stakeholders using clear analogies. We successfully implemented the plan, reducing query latency by 60%. I believe this blend of technical depth and communication allows me to drive projects forward efficiently."

(Multi-dimensional, quantified impact, self-awareness, relevance to role.)

Explanation: 'Have' for possession does not use -ing.

Wrong: 'I can to handle pressure.'
Correct: 'I can handle pressure.'

Explanation: Modals don't take 'to'.

Wrong: 'Myself John, I am a developer.'
Correct: 'My name is John, I am a developer.'

Explanation: Never start an introduction with 'Myself'.

Wrong: 'I am working here since 2 years.'
Correct: 'I have been working here for 2 years.'

Explanation: Use present perfect continuous with 'for' for duration.

Wrong: 'I will revert back to you.'
Correct: 'I will get back to you.'

Explanation: 'Revert back' is redundant.

Wrong: 'Discuss about the issue.'
Correct: 'Discuss the issue.'

Explanation: Discuss means 'talk about', so 'about' is redundant.

Wrong: 'I did a mistake.'
Correct: 'I made a mistake.'

Explanation: We 'make' mistakes, not 'do' them.

Wrong: 'Pass out from college.'
Correct: 'Graduate from college.'

Explanation: 'Pass out' means to faint.

Wrong: 'Pre-pone the meeting.'
Correct: 'Bring the meeting forward.'

Explanation: 'Prepone' is Indian English, not globally recognized.

Wrong: 'Any doubts?'
Correct: 'Any questions?'

Explanation: 'Doubt' implies suspicion, use 'question' instead.
